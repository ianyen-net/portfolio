ShareKit: A Sharing Design System Built for Growth

I designed ShareKit, a sharing system that layers a consistent preview and action model on top of the native ShareSheet, and used it to fix fragmented sharing journeys across NotebookLM, Gemini, and Google Maps.

OVERVIEW

The Problem

Sharing Was Inconsistent Across Google

Sharing is how Google apps grow. A good share brings a new person into the product, because the receiver opens something valuable that a sender chose to send. But every first-party app that wanted more than the default system sheet had built its own bespoke sharing UI. The result was fragmentation: different entry points, different previews, different actions, and a different mental model in every app.

I was asked to design a system that gives every first-party app a consistent, data-driven sharing design, without asking people to relearn a new pattern in each place they share from.

The audit covered YouTube, Maps, Gemini, and Photos. The launch partners were NotebookLM, Gemini, and Google Maps.

WHAT THE RESEARCH SHOWED

The Sharing Journey

Was Broken

Defining the sharing journey helped us identify areas of impact. We framed our problems and design principles around these four stages of journey: Discover, Share, Receive, and Re-engage. Across those stages, the audit surfaced the same problems again and again, in plain language:

01
DISCOVER

Inconsistent entry points

Sharing started from different places, with different affordances, depending on the app.

02
SHARE

No clear success confirmation

After tapping a person, a third-party app, or Copy link, users often could not tell whether the share had actually sent.

03
RECEIVE

Anxiety about ambiguous links

Senders and receivers could not see exactly what was being shared when all they got was a link, so trust dropped at the moment of sending and receiving.

04
RE-ENGAGE

Post-share "dead ends"

After a share, there was often nowhere useful to go next, for the sender or the receiver.

Those four problems became the brief for the system. ShareKit did not need to be a new sharing universe. It needed to fix confirmation, entry, preview, and what happens after.

A MODULAR TURN-KEY APPROACH

ShareKit

And Its Design Principles

ShareKit is a suite of offerings that includes a UX interaction framework, front-end library, and back-end infrastructure across mobile and web. Specifically, the UX framework for mobile is called "Splitti", because it visually splits the interface in half. Instead of replacing the system ShareSheet, it layers a preview and action row on top of it.

That layering is the key architectural decision. The system sheet still handles distribution, ranking, and destinations. Splitti owns what the system sheet does not: a consistent preview, clear attribution, and a small set of actions that mean the same thing in every app.

It was built tokens first, then components, with accessibility built in, following the design principles below:

Clear entry points

Elevate clear CTAs in context to shareable content and avoid hiding behind an overflow menu.

Big previews

Provide previews that accurately reflect what is being sent and what the recipient will see in their messaging app.

PROFILE ATTRIBUTION & IDENTITY

Control

Provide tools that enable self-expression and customization of what is being shared to give users more control.

Display metadata

Display accurate metadata for in-chat previews to increase understanding of what has been shared with you.

PREVIEW, CONTENT DESCRIPTION & ACTION

Personal not universal

The final list of app targets and actions to include in the ShareSheet should be based on individual user's usage and context, rather than universal defaults.

Stress Testing the Design System

Using An AI Tool I Built

Try the Tool ↗

Visualizing components in different design systems

Across Google, each first party app has its own design system and color palette. To ensure Splitti is scalable and compatible with all the different systems, I used Antigravity to build a tool to let other designers and engineers rapidly visualize micro-interactions such as what happens after a button is tapped, and transition states of the ShareKit screens. This allowed for highly iterative visual experimentation with different dynamic themes set on the device OS before committing to code.

Component variants and color palettes

In the same tool, I generated the component variants by mapping out all state changes for the elements based on the selected design system, and to audit existing files to ensure compliance with strict accessibility standards, identifying rogue hex codes or detached instances.

Stress-testing with dynamic content

Rather than using "Lorem Ipsum," I also generated realistic, context-heavy copy in different languages such as German and Chinese, error messages, and edge-case data (like exceptionally long shared content or unexpected inputs) to ensure the UI layout of Splitti and text wrapping hold up under real-world conditions.

Turnkey Works

NotebookLM

NotebookLM shipped artifact sharing on ShareKit on the turnkey approach, with little back-and-forth and no customization required.

This was a 0 to 1 feature. Before ShareKit, NotebookLM had no sharing for artifact sharing at all. Users could generate audio, video, and other outputs inside a notebook, but they could not share any of them with anyone. The output stayed stranded in the notebook. This implementation enabled that experience for the first time.

GEMINI PROMPT SHARING

But Turnkey Doesn't Always Work

App Needed An Extended Tier

Gemini wanted to ship a feature that let users share an image together with the prompt that created it, built for virality. The prompt is not decoration here. Users share the image because of the prompt, so the prompt is part of the content being shared.

Splitti is optimized for media sharing, with a large preview of the media, rather than text. Gemini needed two things the turnkey flow did not offer: first, a choice of whether to include a prompt in the sharing flow at all, and second, the ability to edit the prompt before sharing. That distinction is what made the need legitimate rather than a preference. Including a prompt was a configuration question. Editing text inside the flow was where Splitti genuinely ran out: a media-first sheet has no place for a user to revise text before it goes out.

WHAT GEMINI WANTED TO SUPPORT

The evaluation: legitimate need, validated by research

We tested the cheaper path before committing to a new tier. I designed a turnkey version and a configured version of Splitti that squeezed the preview, the toggle, the edit field, and the targets into one sheet, and ran quick research on it. Most users found it way too busy. The prompt controls fought the media preview for attention, and the sheet felt like editing, not sharing. So we did not walk into the tier decision with a hypothesis. We walked in with evidence.

The key decision: a tier, not an exception

The key decision for Gemini's Prompt Sharing feature was a tier, not an exception. Two bad options were on the table: force Gemini into turnkey, and they ship a compromised feature or fork anyway; or hand them a one-off custom build, and the system rots by exception. The research ruled out a third option, the configured variant, because users had already rejected it as too busy.

The tiers are:

Turnkey

Use Splitti as-is

Configured

Approved variants within the existing model

Extended

New capabilities built on ShareKit's foundations, with system review

What the Extended tier actually contains: the half sheet

Gemini became the first Extended implementation, not a waiver, but a designed, documented path that any future team could use.

What the Extended tier contains, for Gemini, is a half sheet that keeps the media-first logic intact while making room for text:

  • Kept locked: the overlay contract, target ranking and third-party routing, and the visual language.
  • New: a half sheet with a preview of the image being shared, a toggle to share with or without the prompt, and an editable prompt field before sharing.
  • Third-party targets, including WhatsApp and Instagram, surfaced based on the user's device usage, so the sheet ends where that person actually shares.

Why an Extended tier was justified

It works where the configured version did not because the prompt gets its own space, instead of competing with everything else in a single crowded sheet. The stakes were larger than one feature. If Gemini, the most visible AI product at Google, shipped a bespoke share flow, it would not just be one fork. It would be a public precedent that the system could not serve flagship needs, and every team with an edge case would have license to build their own. The cost of losing Gemini was not one adopter. It was the system's authority.

Governance is what keeps Extended from becoming the exception path by another name. Extended is not self-serve. A first-party app brings its use case to a review with our team, where it is evaluated against the same test applied to Gemini: is this a capability gap, or a preference?

Gemini did not launch prompt sharing. They shifted priorities before launch due to technical complexity, not because the approach failed. However, the outcome I wanted to show instead is the decision and the evidence behind it: a capability gap identified, a cheaper configured variant tested and rejected by users as too busy, and a tier designed to keep the client in the system.

Regardless, this doesn't undermine ShareKit's huge successes with the other examples above.

REFLECTION

Learnings

Design for a moving target

Get comfortable with discomfort. In the AI era, the thing you're systematizing changes while you systematize it — for example, Gemini reprioritized before our feature launched. What I'd do differently: measure success by adoption and evidence rather than whether the flagship partner shipped, and develop a clear governance and approval process that allows first-party apps to move fast. The hardest part wasn't designing the system; it was trusting the research we did and the other first-party apps' adoption when the biggest test never launched.

Bend only on evidence

Know when to compromise. The Extended tier was a compromise I'm glad we made, but only because the research earned it. We bent because users showed the turnkey and configured designs failed, not because a partner asked loudly. What I'd do differently: define the bend criteria before the partner conversation, not during it. Compromise decided in advance is a principle, but when it's negotiated in the room, it becomes a precedent. NotebookLM and Google Maps taught the flip side: turnkey worked because we held the line on simplicity. Because of Gemini, we now have a set of principles to evaluate against partners' requests.
