CORE CHALLENGE

A Product Designed For Completeness

But Is Rarely Complete

The effectiveness of Google's connected experiences (Maps, Calendar, Assistant) depends entirely on the richness of contact data stored by users. Without an address, Maps can't surface a contact's location. Without a birthday, Calendar can't trigger a reminder. Contacts is the backbone of many features across Google.

The gap between what Contacts was designed to do and what users actually did with it had become a strategic problem for the product. Improving contact data quality required understanding and removing the barriers to editing.

EXPECTATION REALITY
EXPECTATION REALITY

To address this gap, I led the redesign of the "Rich Info" initiative, focused on identifying the root causes of the lack of contact info saved by users. The solution involved making it significantly easier for users to add contact information with a complete overhaul of the Contacts Editor across Android, Wear OS, and desktop, transforming a dense, form-based workflow into a streamlined, progressive editing experience.

XFN WORKSHOP OUTPUT

Three Hypotheses

Only Two Worth Pursuing

Through an empathy exercise with Product, Engineering, and Design, we synthesized friction moments into three core hypotheses.

01
HYPOTHESIS 1 · USABILITY

Feels Like a Tax Form

The current editing experience was cluttered and high-friction, with too many fields presented upfront.

02
HYPOTHESIS 2 · MOTIVATION

Users Don't See the Benefit

Users didn't clearly understand why entering contact information was worthwhile. For example, most users didn't know that adding an address enables searching for that contact's place directly in Google Maps.

03
HYPOTHESIS 3 · DATA

Users Are Missing Critical Details

This was disproven as an existing survey showed 86% of participants were comfortable sharing info about their contacts.

THE CONFLICT

My PM and I Disagreed

On The Root Cause

My PM and I initially diverged on which hypothesis mattered most. Product believed the core issue was lack of value proposition vs I believed it was usability. We compromised on a two-track validation plan that balanced speed, rigor and cross-functional priorities.

PM'S VIEW

"LET'S SHIP IT AND GET REAL WORLD DATA"

The core problem was lack of value proposition. Users simply didn't understand why adding more contact information was useful. Show them the benefits, and they'll act.

MY VIEW

"WE SHOULDN'T SHIP A HYPOTHESIS WITHOUT VALIDATION"

Informed by early usability reviews, the core problem was the editing experience itself. It felt overwhelming and high-friction (like a tax form, not a tool).

VALIDATION PLAN

From Conflicting Priorities

To a Shared Validation Plan

To move forward, we compromised on a two-track validation plan: run a lightweight A/B experiment on value props while conducting qualitative usability research in parallel. This let us test both hypotheses simultaneously without delaying either workstream.

FINAL DESIGN

Designing a Less Is More

Contact Editor

To address the friction, I explored multiple approaches to field layout and progressive disclosure. Through iterative testing and team discussion, we landed on three core improvements that transformed the editing experience.

01

Progressive Field Creation: fields appear only when chosen

We replaced the long list of empty input fields with action-based entry points: Add phone, Add email, Add address. Fields only appear when users explicitly choose to add them. This eliminated the overwhelming "tax form" feeling while keeping all data types fully accessible.

9 → 4 visible fields on open
02

Data-driven deprecation: usage data determined what to cut

Using product usage data, we reorganized the editor around the fields users actually interacted with. Common fields were surfaced prominently. Fields with extremely low usage below 0.01% daily usage were deprecated entirely. Deciding what to remove was as important as deciding what to add, and required careful cross-functional alignment.

Deprecated fields <0.01% daily usage
03

Progressive disclosure: secondary fields on user demand only

The previous design automatically revealed secondary fields (additional phone labels, secondary email rows) during editing. This created complexity users hadn't asked for. The new design surfaces additional fields only when the user explicitly requests them, keeping the active state focused and predictable.

+29–37% increase in usability ratings post-launch

DESIGN SYSTEM

Built for Contacts

Designed to Scale

Rather than designing one-off components, I structured the system as modular building blocks that could compose a wide variety of contact data types. The goal wasn't just to solve for Contacts — it was to create a flexible framework that could contribute back to Google's central design system.

The key decisions that made it adoptable: scalable component properties defined upfront, stateful behaviors consistent across data types, and variant configurations for each platform surface — rather than separate components per platform.

The component system was later adopted by Google Wallet and Magic Cue for their editing interfaces, validating the upfront investment in scalable architecture. What made adoption possible wasn't just good components; it was that each component had defined scalable properties and behaviors, making adaptation for new data types straightforward without redesign from scratch.

REFLECTION

Learnings

Friction beats motivation every time

The A/B test proved that communicating value cannot overcome a high-friction interface. When ease and comprehension compete, ease wins — every added step needs a strong justification.

The PM disagreement made the work better

Instead of deferring to the PM's hypothesis or pushing mine unilaterally, our two-track plan let the data decide and built shared ownership of the final direction.

Designing for adoptability compounds impact

Investing in a modular, scalable component architecture meant our editor patterns were later adopted by Google Wallet and Magic Cue. Designing for future adopters isn't scope creep — it's leverage.

Removal is design too

Deprecating fields with sub-0.01% daily usage was as impactful as the new patterns we introduced. Knowing what to cut — backed by data — is core to simplifying complex workflows.
