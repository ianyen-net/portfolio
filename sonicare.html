Philips Sonicare Connected App Relaunch

BUSINESS OPPORTUNITY

AI Powered Toothbrush Experience

Advances in our hardware (specifically the new brush) gave us the ability to show accurate brushing coverage by segment. There is a clear business need to integrate this feature into the app to showcase the value of the new technology.

Additionally, low app store ratings and adoption rates suggest that users are not satisfied with the current app experience. To address this, we aimed to redesign the UX to improve usability, engagement, and overall satisfaction.

USER FRUSTRATION

Our Users Don't See the Value in Our Product

Did our previous app deliver on its promise to users? Unfortunately, the answer was no. We conducted user research to better understand the challenges our customers faced, and several key issues emerged:

  • Lack of perceived value: Many users felt the toothbrush alone provided the benefits, and didn't see a clear reason to use the app.
  • No visible results: Users reported no noticeable improvements in their oral health care (OHC), leading to disengagement.
  • Poor usability: The interface was clunky and the overall user experience was frustrating.

In short, users didn't feel the app supported them in building the skills needed to improve their oral health care effectively.

I spent a week with a cross-functional team (a UX researcher, content strategist, PM, TPM, and myself) mapping every user pain point and designing solutions to help people build better brushing habits.

The conceptual framework we used was a cyclic process of:
Learn → Brush → Realize

"I only get my brushing results in averages but there's no visualization to help me understand the data"

Our users often struggled to brush correctly and thoroughly with the toothbrush. To address this, our solution aims to help users improve their brushing by increasing awareness of areas that were brushed incorrectly or insufficiently.

I always define principles before doing design work — they keep the team and me focused on users and on our original intent:

  • Personalized: feedback based on their data, not generic advice they could find online.
  • Delightful: turn a mundane daily routine into a positive experience.
  • Useful for everyone: whether someone brushes meticulously or is just starting out, they get something out of the app.

WORKING WITH ENG TO UNDERSTAND WHAT'S TECHNICALLY POSSIBLE

1. Mapping Data to Visuals

The first step was selecting a visual format that aligned with both the structure of the brushing data and the intended functionality.

I collaborated closely with engineers to understand how the data was structured and to clarify what interactions and insights the visualization needed to support. For example, the table on the left shows raw data from a single brushing session.

If a user brushes for a total of 1 minute and 30 seconds, that time is broken down into 16 segments. For instance, “TRI” indicates the Top-Right-Inner surface.

Based on this segmented data, I mapped each data point to graphic marks on a mouth map, allowing users to visually see where and how effectively they brushed.

2. Contextualizing Visuals into Information

To make the visualization meaningful, I enriched the encoded mouth map with contextual cues, such as highlighted borders and the number of seconds spent on each segment. This transformed the raw visual into interpretable information.

These enhancements made the mouth map intuitive and informative, enabling users to compare brushing coverage across segments, identify patterns, and better understand their brushing behavior based on clear evidence.

3. User Feedback and Iterative Improvements

In this phase, I focused on aligning the visualization design with UX principles, ensuring that every visual element, motion, color, and interaction served to enhance clarity, usability, and user trust.

User feedback indicated that:

  • Seconds over percentages: Users preferred seeing brushing time in seconds rather than percentages, as they didn't want to calculate it themselves.
  • Line-only visuals lacked clarity: We addressed this by overlaying the data on a full mouth map for better spatial context.
  • Accuracy became a key concern: Users emphasized the importance of data accuracy. After consulting with the engineering team, we learned the algorithm for detecting chewing surfaces was unreliable, so we removed that element from the visualization.
  • Legends were preferred: Including a legend reduced visual clutter and made it unnecessary to label each segment with “s” (seconds), improving overall readability.

BALANCING STAKEHOLDER VS PRODUCT NEEDS

3-Month vs. Yearly Data View

During the design process, a key point of debate arose between the PM and stakeholders regarding how to display brushing data over time.

  • Stakeholders advocated for a 3-month (quarterly) view, aligned with an insurance rewards program that incentivizes users for meeting oral health goals on a quarterly basis.
  • The PM preferred a yearly view, as it follows a more conventional data reporting structure and provides long-term insight.

The Solution: To address both needs, I designed a yearly view segmented into clearly defined quarterly blocks, allowing users to track their progress across the full year while highlighting quarter-specific milestones for reward eligibility.
